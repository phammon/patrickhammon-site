Home Insurance, Flood Zones, and HOA Considerations in Central Florida

Check Florida wind mitigation, four-point inspection requests, roof-age rules, hurricane deductibles, flood information, HOA documents, and CDD assessments before buying.

By Patrick Hammon · Coldwell Banker Ellison Realty · Published November 24, 2026

Before you commit to a Central Florida home, get answers from the people who set the insurance terms and manage the property's community obligations. A roof that looks fine during a showing may still prompt an insurer to ask for records or an inspection. A FEMA map is useful, but it is not an insurance quote. And an HOA assessment is not the same thing as a community development district assessment.

Use the exact property address and check these items early enough to follow up during your contract timelines.

Ask the insurer which inspections it needs

Florida buyers commonly hear about two different inspections:

Inspection What it documents Why it may be requested
Wind mitigation inspection Construction features that may reduce wind damage, documented on Florida's Uniform Mitigation Verification form. An insurer reviews the report to determine whether policy discounts or other treatment may apply. A report does not guarantee a discount.
Four-point inspection The roof, electrical, plumbing, and heating/cooling systems. An insurer may use it to assess an older or otherwise specified home. Requirements vary by insurer and application. Citizens currently requires one for certain owner, dwelling, and mobile-home applications when the property is over 20 years old.
Buyer home inspection The home's overall observed condition and systems, under the inspector's scope and agreement. This is for the buyer's due diligence; it is not a substitute for an insurer's required inspection or coverage decision.

Ask your insurance agent which reports the carrier will accept, who may complete them, how recent they must be, and whether a new inspection is needed. A wind mitigation report and a four-point inspection answer different questions; do not assume one replaces the other.

Roof age matters, but it is not the whole underwriting decision

Ask the seller for roof permits, replacement records, inspection reports, and any wind mitigation documents. Confirm the roof's age and material with records where possible; a verbal estimate is not the same as a permit or insurer-accepted inspection.

Florida law limits an insurer's ability to refuse or nonrenew a homeowners policy solely because of roof age. Under the 2026 statute, a roof under 15 years old cannot be the sole reason for refusal or nonrenewal. For a roof at least 15 years old, the insurer must allow an inspection by an authorized inspector before requiring replacement; the law also bars refusal solely because of age when that inspection finds at least five years of useful life remaining. This rule does not guarantee an insurer will offer coverage: condition, documentation, underwriting rules, and other factors still matter.

Read the hurricane deductible in the policy

Ask for the declarations page and the applicable hurricane-deductible endorsement. Confirm the deductible amount, how it is calculated, and when it applies. A percentage deductible can produce a different dollar amount than a flat deductible, so compare the policy wording—not just the quoted premium. Ask the insurance professional to explain how the hurricane deductible differs from the policy's other deductibles.

Florida's Department of Financial Services provides a consumer guide explaining the state's hurricane-deductible rules. The policy controls the coverage for a particular home.

Check flood information separately

Search the exact address in FEMA's Flood Map Service Center and save the map product and effective date you reviewed. FEMA describes the site as the official public source for its flood-hazard information and notes that maps can change.

Most homeowners policies do not cover flood damage; flood insurance is a separate policy. A lender may require flood insurance for a home in a high-risk flood area when the mortgage is federally backed. A map result does not predict whether a home will flood or tell you what a policy will cost. Ask your lender and a licensed insurance professional about the specific property and coverage options.

Separate HOA obligations from CDD assessments

If an HOA applies, request its current governing documents, budget, assessment schedule, rules, and sale-related disclosures. Look for regular or special assessments, maintenance responsibilities, architectural approval, rental or use restrictions, and any fees due when ownership transfers. Florida law provides for HOA assessments and estoppel certificates, but the recorded documents and current account determine what applies to a particular parcel.

A CDD is a separate type of special-purpose local government. Florida law authorizes district taxes and special assessments in specified circumstances; some assessments can appear on the county tax roll. Do not add a CDD assessment and an HOA charge together under one vague “community fee.” Identify each payee and line item on the property-tax notice and current documents.

Buyer due-diligence checklist

  1. Request the current insurance declarations page, if available, and ask for an address-specific quote.
  2. Ask the insurer which reports it requires: wind mitigation, four-point, roof, or another inspection.
  3. Verify roof age and replacement documentation; do not rely on age alone to predict insurability.
  4. Compare the hurricane deductible and other deductibles in writing.
  5. Search the address in FEMA's map service and ask the insurer about separate flood coverage.
  6. Obtain HOA documents, current assessments, rules, and transfer or estoppel information.
  7. Check the current tax bill for CDD or other non-ad valorem assessment lines.
  8. Confirm all due-diligence deadlines with your agent, lender, insurer, and closing professional.

If a property is under consideration, start a buyer conversation to organize address-specific questions for the insurer, association, district, and inspection professionals. Whether I can represent a particular property or transaction must be confirmed before proceeding.
