Buying a Home Near The Villages: What to Know

Compare homes near The Villages by county, housing type, district, HOA, amenity access, insurance, and property-specific costs.

By Patrick Hammon · Coldwell Banker Ellison Realty · Published November 24, 2026

“Near The Villages” can describe very different properties. The Villages itself spans Lake, Sumter, and Marion counties. Nearby searches may include Oxford, Wildwood, Lady Lake, Summerfield, Belleview, and Ocala, but a mailing address or familiar community name does not tell you which government, district, association, or service provider applies to a home.

The practical rule is simple: compare the exact properties, not just their area labels. Verify the parcel, then use its documents and current written estimates to understand the full cost and rules.

Start with the address, county, and property type

Before comparing monthly costs, record the street address and parcel identification number for each home. Look up the parcel with the appropriate county property appraiser and check municipal boundaries with the relevant city or county map. The Villages’ official FAQ lists home series such as Patio Villas, Courtyard Villas, Cottage Homes, Verandas, Designer, and Premier homes. In surrounding areas, use current listings to compare the property types actually available; do not assume every community has every type.

If a home is outside a municipal water or sewer service area, ask the provider and seller about the property's actual water, wastewater, road, and maintenance arrangements. Do not infer these from a ZIP code or a nearby subdivision.

CDD, HOA, and amenity charges are different questions

These terms are sometimes grouped together in casual conversation, but they describe different arrangements:

Item What it generally means What to check for the specific home
Community development district (CDD) A special-purpose local government created under Florida law. A district may finance or maintain authorized infrastructure and may levy taxes or special assessments. Identify the district on its map; read its budget, bond and assessment information, and the parcel's current tax notice.
Homeowners' association (HOA) An association operating a community under recorded covenants and governing documents. Florida law distinguishes an HOA from a CDD. Request the declaration, current budget, assessment details, rules, disclosures, and any estoppel information available for the sale.
Amenity or recreation access Access may depend on a separate fee, governing document, service agreement, or district arrangement. It is not automatically included because a home is nearby or marketed with a broad community name. Verify the current fee, who may use the facilities, what the payment covers, and whether it applies to this parcel.
Property taxes and other assessments County property-tax bills can include ad valorem taxes and separately listed non-ad valorem assessments. Review the current parcel bill and ask the tax collector or closing professional about any item you do not understand.

The Villages publishes current amenity and estimated ownership-cost information, and its Community Development Districts publish district maps and finance information. Treat online averages as examples, not a quote for a resale home. District boundaries, amounts, and access terms should be confirmed for the address.

Build a property-specific monthly budget

For each finalist, write down:

  • Principal, interest, and any mortgage-related charges from your lender.
  • Current property-tax bill and any exemptions that may or may not apply to you.
  • Homeowners and, if relevant, flood-insurance quotes for the exact home.
  • HOA or other association dues, CDD assessments, amenity charges, and utility costs that apply.
  • Maintenance items such as roof, HVAC, septic, well, lawn, and pest service where relevant.
  • One-time costs, including inspections, association documents, repairs, and closing items.

Keep estimates separate from confirmed charges. A tax estimate based on a previous owner’s exemptions, an online amenity average, or an insurance quote for a different roof can make two homes look more alike than they are.

A practical homebuying checklist

  1. Get the exact address and parcel ID; identify the county and whether the property is inside municipal boundaries.
  2. Read the current property-tax notice. Check each tax authority and non-ad valorem line instead of relying on the neighborhood name.
  3. Identify any CDD and obtain current district, bond, and assessment information.
  4. Request the HOA or community documents, current dues, rules, and sale-related disclosures.
  5. Confirm amenity or recreation access and fees in writing; do not assume nearby homes have the same access.
  6. Get property-specific insurance quotes early. Check roof documentation, wind mitigation information, and FEMA flood-map products with the right professionals.
  7. Ask about water, sewer, septic, well, roads, drainage, and utility providers as applicable.
  8. Visit at the times you would travel and verify routes and services yourself.
  9. Ask your lender, inspector, title professional, and attorney questions within their respective roles.
  10. Before discussing representation, confirm that the specific property and transaction are within the applicable brokerage arrangements.

Make the search fit your priorities

Choose a few must-haves—such as total monthly budget, home type, travel routes, maintenance needs, or access to particular services—and use the same checklist for every address.
